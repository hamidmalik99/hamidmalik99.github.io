Business Broker Recently Sold Page: Show Deals You've Closed

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

A business broker recently sold page lists deals your firm closed, with a short entry for each showing the sector, broad region, timing, buyer type and broker, plus figures only with permission. Get written permission for every entry, avoid details that identify the business, and only make claims you can back up with records.

Key takeaways

  • A recently sold page shows sellers you close deals in their sector and region.
  • Get written permission for every entry and anonymize by default.
  • Check that sector, location and timing together don't identify the business.
  • The FTC requires a reasonable basis for ad claims; the CAP Code requires documentary evidence for objective claims.
  • Use one filterable page, link deals to brokers and sector pages, and keep it current.

An owner thinking about selling wants one thing from a broker's website above all: proof that you actually close deals. A business broker recently sold page is the most direct way to show it. Done well, it gives sellers confidence that you can sell a business like theirs, and gives buyers a sense of the deals you handle. Done badly, it breaks a former client's confidentiality or makes claims you can't back up. This guide covers what to include, how to protect confidentiality, the US and UK advertising rules your claims must follow, and how to structure the page so it keeps working as you close more deals.

Why a business broker recently sold page matters

Most seller-focused pages on a broker's website describe what you'll do. A recently sold page shows what you've done. For an owner comparing brokers, a list of closed deals in their sector or region is often more persuasive than any description of your process.

It also helps your website in practical ways:

  • each sold deal is a short, specific piece of content about a sector and region
  • sector filters let an owner find deals like theirs in seconds
  • a growing list shows that the firm is active

What to show for each sold business

Keep each entry short and consistent. Common elements:

  • Sector, described broadly enough not to identify the business (for example, "Commercial cleaning company")
  • Region, such as a state, county or metro area rather than a town
  • When it sold, by quarter or year
  • Buyer type, such as individual buyer, competitor, private equity or management buyout
  • A short summary: what made the business attractive and how the sale came together
  • Deal figures, only if you have permission to share them, such as asking and sale price ranges or how long the sale took
  • The broker who handled it, linked to their profile

Confidentiality comes first

Many sellers want the sale kept quiet even after closing, because of staff, customers, suppliers or the terms of the deal itself. Your engagement agreement and any confidentiality obligations will set out what you can disclose. Before publishing anything:

  • Get written permission from the seller, and the buyer if the deal involves them, for each entry
  • Agree what can be shown: sector, region, timing and any figures
  • Anonymize by default. "Specialist HVAC contractor, Midwest" is often enough
  • Avoid accidental identification. A rare sector plus a small town plus a sale year can identify a business even without its name
  • Check photos. Don't use images of premises, vehicles or signage unless the seller has agreed

The advertising rules your claims must follow

A recently sold page is advertising, and the claims on it need to be true and provable.

United States

The FTC's Advertising FAQs: A Guide for Small Business explains that under the FTC Act, advertising must be truthful and non-deceptive, advertisers must have evidence to back up their claims, and ads can't be unfair. It says that before a company runs an ad, it must have a "reasonable basis" for its claims, meaning objective evidence that supports them, and that the FTC looks at both express and implied claims, judged from the point of view of a reasonable consumer. It also notes that every state has its own consumer protection laws.

In practice, that means:

  • only list deals you actually closed, and that you were the broker on
  • keep records for every figure you publish, such as sale prices or time to close
  • be careful with summary claims like "we sell most businesses within six months"; you need data to back them up, and they shouldn't imply a typical result you can't support

United Kingdom

The CAP Code's rules on misleading advertising say marketing communications must not materially mislead, and that marketers must hold documentary evidence to prove claims consumers are likely to regard as objective. The same section says testimonials must be genuine, with documentary evidence and contact details held for the person who gave them, and must not be used without permission.

None of this is legal advice. Check the rules that apply where you work, including any rules from your professional body, and take advice if you're unsure.

Structuring the page

One page, filterable

A single "Recently sold" page with filters for sector, region and year works for most firms. Owners can find deals like theirs quickly, and the page grows as you close more.

Short entries with optional detail

Show a short card for each deal. Where the seller agrees to more detail, link to a fuller write-up: the challenge, how you marketed the business, how buyers were found and how the deal closed.

Links to the rest of your site

  • link sector filters to your sector pages
  • link each deal's broker to their profile
  • end the page with a clear next step for sellers, such as a confidential valuation request; my guide to the business broker valuation page covers that page

Keep it current

Add new deals as they close, and review older entries periodically. A page whose newest deal is three years old suggests the firm has gone quiet.

Before and after: a sold entry

Here's an invented example.

Before: "Another successful sale! Thanks to our amazing team for a record-breaking result."

After: "Commercial cleaning company, Southeast. Sold Q2 to a strategic buyer in the same sector. Long-standing contracts and a stable management team attracted several interested buyers. Handled by [broker name]."

The second version is specific, avoids unprovable claims like "record-breaking", and doesn't identify the business. Every detail here is made up; use your own deals, with permission.

Common mistakes

  • Publishing without permission. Even an anonymized entry should be agreed with the seller.
  • Identifying the business by accident. Check that sector, location and timing together don't point to one company.
  • Claims you can't prove. "Highest prices in the region" or "record sale" need evidence.
  • Including deals you didn't close. Only list deals your firm was the broker on.
  • Letting the page go stale. Add deals as they close.

Frequently asked questions

What should a business broker show on a recently sold page?

For each deal, show the sector described broadly, the region, when it sold by quarter or year, the buyer type, a short summary of what made the business attractive and the broker who handled it. Include sale prices or timelines only if the seller has agreed and you have records to support them.

Do I need permission to list a sold business on my website?

You should get written permission from the seller, and the buyer where relevant, for every entry, and follow any confidentiality terms in your engagement agreement. Many sellers want the sale kept quiet, so anonymize by default and agree exactly what can be shown before you publish anything.

Can I say we sell businesses faster or for more than other brokers?

Only if you can prove it. The FTC says advertisers need a reasonable basis, meaning objective evidence, for express and implied claims, and the UK CAP Code requires documentary evidence for objective claims. Avoid phrases like record sale or highest prices unless you hold the data to back them up.

How do I stop a sold listing from identifying the business?

Describe the sector broadly, use a state, county or metro area rather than a town, show the sale year or quarter rather than a date, and avoid photos of premises, vehicles or signage. Then check whether the combination of details could still point to one company, especially in small or specialist markets.
