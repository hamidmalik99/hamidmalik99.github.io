Fractional CFO Pricing Page: How to Show Fees on Your Website

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

A fractional CFO pricing page should explain how you charge, show honest starting prices, ranges or at least your model, list what each level includes, state minimum terms and notice, and explain what affects the fee. Make 'from' prices genuinely available, state VAT status next to prices in the UK, and end with a clear route to a tailored proposal.

Key takeaways

  • A pricing page helps founders check budget, model and commitment before booking a call.
  • You can show starting prices, ranges by tier or just your model, as long as what you publish is accurate.
  • The CAP Code says 'from' prices must not mislead by exaggerating what clients are likely to get.
  • VAT-exclusive prices in the UK must be accompanied by a prominent statement of the VAT payable.
  • The FTC treats price as a material claim, so published figures should reflect what clients actually pay.

Should a fractional CFO firm publish its prices? Founders who land on your site often want a rough idea of cost before they book a call. Others worry that publishing prices invites price-shopping or undersells complex work. A good fractional CFO pricing page sits in between: it explains how you charge, gives honest starting points or ranges, and makes it easy to get a proper quote. This guide covers how to structure the page, how to present retainers, onboarding and project work, and the UK and US advertising rules that apply to prices and "from" claims.

Why a pricing page helps even if you don't publish exact fees

A founder comparing fractional CFOs usually wants to know three things before talking to anyone:

  • Is this in my budget, roughly?
  • How does the arrangement work: monthly, by the day, by project?
  • What am I committing to, and can I stop?

If your site answers none of these, you rely on the prospect booking a call just to find out whether you're affordable. A pricing page that answers them filters out poor fits and gives good-fit prospects the confidence to get in touch.

There's no single right answer. What matters is that whatever you publish is accurate and clearly qualified.

What to include on the page

How you charge

Explain your model in plain English. Typical structures include a monthly retainer for ongoing support, a set number of days per month, a fixed-fee onboarding or diagnostic phase, and separately priced projects such as fundraising support or a finance system implementation. Say which you use and why.

What each level includes

If you use tiers, describe them by the client's situation rather than abstract names: "early-stage, pre-revenue", "growing, preparing to raise", "established, board reporting". For each, list what's included, such as forecasting, management accounts review, board packs, investor updates and days or hours per month, and what isn't.

Commitment and terms

State the minimum term, notice period and how you handle changes in scope. Founders worry about being locked in, so clarity here builds trust.

What affects the price

List the main factors: transaction volume, number of entities, the state of the books at the start, reporting requirements and how much hands-on bookkeeping or system work is needed. This explains why your figures are ranges and prepares prospects for the scoping conversation.

A clear next step

End with a short form or booking link: "Tell us about your business and we'll send a tailored proposal." My guide to what a fractional CFO website should include covers the enquiry form in more detail.

The rules on prices and "from" claims

Prices on your website are advertising claims. Both the UK and the US have rules that apply.

United Kingdom

The CAP Code's section on misleading advertising includes rules on prices. Price statements must not mislead by omission, undue emphasis or distortion. Price claims such as "up to" and "from" must not mislead by exaggerating the availability or amount of benefits likely to be obtained.

So if you advertise "from £1,500 a month", that price should be genuinely available for the service described, not a stripped-down package almost nobody buys.

The same section deals with VAT. Quoted prices must include non-optional taxes, but VAT-exclusive prices may be given if everyone the price is clearly addressed to pays no VAT or can recover it. In that case the price must be accompanied by a prominent statement of the amount or rate of VAT payable. Not every client will be VAT-registered or able to recover VAT, so if you quote prices excluding VAT, say so clearly next to the figure.

The section also says marketing must not mislead by omitting significant limitations and qualifications, and qualifications must be presented clearly. If a starting price assumes a minimum term or excludes onboarding, say so near the price, not in small print elsewhere.

United States

The FTC's Advertising FAQs for small business explain that under the FTC Act, advertising must be truthful and non-deceptive and advertisers must have evidence to back up their claims. The FAQ lists price among the examples of claims that are "material", meaning important to a consumer's decision. Starting prices and ranges should reflect what clients actually pay.

These rules depend on the facts, so check the current guidance and take legal advice if you're unsure. This isn't legal advice.

Before and after: a pricing section

Here's an invented example of a pricing section written two ways.

Before: "Affordable CFO services from just £499! Contact us for a quote."

After: "How we charge. Most clients start with a fixed-fee 60-day diagnostic, then move to a monthly retainer. Typical monthly retainers: £2,000–£3,500 for early-stage businesses (two days a month), £3,500–£6,000 for businesses preparing to raise (three to four days a month). Fundraising support and system projects are priced separately. Terms: three-month minimum, then one month's notice. All prices exclude VAT at the standard rate. We'll confirm your fee in a written proposal after a short scoping call."

The second version is specific, explains what drives the price and makes the qualifications clear. The figures here are illustrative only; use your own.

Common pricing page mistakes

  • A teaser price nobody pays. A very low "from" figure might attract clicks, but it erodes trust on the first call and can fall foul of the CAP Code rule on "from" prices.
  • Hiding the pricing page. If founders have to dig for it, many won't. Link to it from your main navigation and from each service page.
  • Jargon-heavy package names. "Growth Accelerator Plus" tells a founder nothing. Describe each level by the business stage it suits and what's included.
  • No mention of what's extra. If fundraising support, system implementations or additional days are charged separately, say so on the page.
  • Out-of-date figures. Review your prices and terms whenever your fees change, and note the date you last updated them.

Frequently asked questions

Should fractional CFOs publish their prices?

You don't have to publish exact fees, but explaining how you charge helps. Some firms show starting prices or ranges by stage, plus the model: monthly retainers, days per month, onboarding phases and separately priced projects. Whatever you publish should be accurate and clearly qualified, with a route to a tailored proposal.

Can I advertise prices excluding VAT?

Under the UK CAP Code, quoted prices must include non-optional taxes, but VAT-exclusive prices may be given if everyone the price is clearly addressed to pays no VAT or can recover it. In that case the price must be accompanied by a prominent statement of the amount or rate of VAT payable.

What should a fractional CFO pricing page include?

A plain explanation of how you charge, starting prices or ranges if you publish them, what each level includes and excludes, minimum term and notice period, the factors that affect price, any VAT statement, and a clear next step such as a proposal request form or booking link.

Are 'from' prices allowed on a finance firm website?

Yes, if they're not misleading. The CAP Code says 'up to' and 'from' price claims must not mislead by exaggerating the availability or amount of benefits likely to be obtained, so a 'from' price should be genuinely available for the service described. In the US, the FTC requires advertising to be truthful and backed by evidence.
