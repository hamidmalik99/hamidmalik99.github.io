Engineering Project Portfolio: Present Projects That Win Work

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

An engineering project portfolio that wins work gives each project its own page with a plain headline, a facts table, the challenge, your approach, the result and labelled photos. Add filters by sector, service and location, use HTML images with descriptive alt text, get client permission, and link every project to your enquiry form.

Key takeaways

  • Clients want to see similar projects and your exact role, not just a gallery of photos.
  • Use the same structure on every project page: headline, facts, challenge, approach, result and photos.
  • Google indexes images in HTML img elements, not CSS backgrounds, and uses alt text and nearby text to understand them.
  • Ask clients before publishing projects, addresses or drawings, and use photos you have the rights to.
  • In the UK, the CAP Code requires evidence that testimonials are genuine and permission to use them.

Builders, architects and developers judge an engineering consultancy by the work it has done. Your engineering project portfolio is where they look, and on most engineering websites it's either a gallery of unlabelled photos or a PDF capability statement nobody opens on a phone. A portfolio that wins work does more than show pictures. It shows the problem, what your firm did and why it mattered, organised so a busy client can find a project like theirs in seconds. This guide covers how to structure project pages, what to write, how to handle photos and client permission, and how to make your portfolio findable on Google.

What clients look for in an engineering project portfolio

A builder deciding whether to send you a job, or an architect assembling a consultant team, is usually asking:

  • Have they done work like mine, in size, type and location?
  • What exactly was their role?
  • Did they deal with anything difficult, such as poor ground, a tight site or a heritage building?
  • Are they reliable to work with?

A wall of photos doesn't answer these. Short, structured project pages do.

How to structure each project page

Use the same structure for every project so readers can scan quickly.

1. A plain headline

Describe what it is and where: "Two-storey rear extension and steel frame, Brunswick" or "Retaining wall and drainage design for a sloping residential subdivision". Avoid project code names that mean nothing to outsiders.

2. Project facts in a short table

Field Example
Location Suburb or town
Sector Residential, commercial, industrial, civil
Client type Homeowner, builder, architect, developer, council
Our role Structural design, inspection, certification, civil design
Key elements Steel frame, retaining wall, footings, remedial works
Year Year completed

3. The challenge

Two or three sentences on what made the job interesting or difficult: reactive clay, limited access, an existing structure in poor condition, a tight programme.

4. What you did

Explain your approach in plain English, with enough technical detail to show competence but not so much that a builder or homeowner gives up. Mention how you worked with the architect, builder or certifier.

5. The result

What was delivered and how it helped the client. Describe outcomes accurately. Never inflate them.

6. Photos and drawings

Construction-stage photos, finished photos and, where the client agrees, simplified drawings. Site photos of steelwork and footings often say more to a builder than a polished finished shot.

Organise the portfolio so people find what they need

  • Filters by sector and service. Let visitors narrow to "residential extensions" or "retaining walls" in one click.
  • Location tags. These help clients see you've worked near them, and support the area pages on your site. My guide to local SEO for structural engineers explains why genuine local content matters.
  • Featured projects first. Pick three to six strong, recent projects for the top of the page and your homepage.
  • Links from service pages. Your retaining wall service page should show your retaining wall projects.

Photos: quality, speed and Google Images

Project photos can bring visitors from Google Images, but only if they're set up properly. Google's image SEO best practices include:

  • Use standard HTML image elements. Google finds images in the src attribute of <img> elements and says it doesn't index CSS background images. Galleries built entirely from background images won't help you in image search.
  • Write descriptive alt text. Google calls alt text the most important attribute for image metadata. It also helps people using screen readers. "Steel portal frame for warehouse extension, Dandenong" is useful; a string of keywords is not, and Google warns that keyword stuffing in alt text can cause a site to be seen as spam.
  • Use descriptive filenames. Google says filenames give light clues about the subject, so steel-frame-rear-extension-brunswick.jpg is better than IMG00023.JPG.
  • Place images near relevant text. Google uses captions and surrounding content to understand images, which is another reason to write a proper description for each project.
  • Balance quality and speed. Google notes that sharp images are more appealing, but images are often the largest contributor to page size. Resize and compress photos, and use responsive images so phones don't download desktop-sized files.

Permission, confidentiality and testimonials

Before publishing a project, check:

  • Client permission. Some clients, especially developers and commercial owners, may not want their project, address or drawings online. Ask, and agree what can be shown. Use the suburb rather than the full address for residential work.
  • Photo rights. Use photos you took or have permission to use. If an architect or photographer took them, ask and credit them.
  • Drawings. Simplified or partial drawings are often enough to show your work without giving away detail the client would prefer to keep private.

If you add a client quote, UK firms should note the CAP Code rules on testimonials. Marketers must hold documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine and hold contact details for the person who gave it, and must not feature a testimonial without permission. Australian firms should apply the same standard: genuine quotes, used with permission. This isn't legal advice.

Before and after: a project summary

Here's an invented example of the same project written two ways.

Before: "Residential project, Brunswick. [Six unlabelled photos]"

After: "Rear extension with steel portal frame, Brunswick. A builder asked us to design a steel frame for a two-storey rear extension on a narrow terrace block, with limited crane access and an existing party wall in poor condition. We designed a lightweight steel frame that could be lifted in sections by hand, with new footings set away from the party wall, and inspected the steel before the slab pour. Our role: structural design and inspection. Completed: 2026."

The second version tells a builder exactly what you did, shows you can handle awkward sites, and gives Google real text to understand the page and its photos.

Frequently asked questions

What should an engineering firm's project portfolio include?

A page for each project with a plain headline, a facts table covering location, sector, client type, your role and key elements, a short description of the challenge, your approach and the result, plus labelled photos and, with permission, simplified drawings. Add filters by sector, service and location and link each project to your enquiry form.

How can engineering project photos rank in Google Images?

Google's image guidance says to use standard HTML img elements rather than CSS backgrounds, write descriptive alt text without keyword stuffing, use descriptive filenames, place images near relevant text and captions, and keep images fast with compression and responsive sizes. A proper written description on each project page helps Google understand the photos.

Do I need client permission to show a project on my website?

It's good practice to ask, especially for commercial and development work where clients may not want projects, addresses or drawings online. Agree what can be shown, use the suburb rather than a full residential address, and only use photos you took or have permission to use, crediting the photographer where appropriate.

Can engineering firms use client testimonials in their portfolio?

Yes, if they're genuine and permitted. In the UK, the CAP Code says marketers must hold documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine, hold contact details for the person who gave it, and not use a testimonial without permission. Firms elsewhere should apply the same standard. Check current guidance if unsure.
