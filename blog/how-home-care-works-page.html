How Home Care Works Page: Explain Your Service to Families

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A how home care works page explains, in plain English, how families arrange care with your agency: the routes through the council or privately, how funding is assessed, your process from first call to care plan and reviews, how carers are matched, prices or quotes, and how to contact you out of hours.

Key takeaways

  • Many families read NHS guidance on homecare before they find your website, so your page should fit with it.
  • The NHS lists questions families should ask agencies; answering them on your page builds trust early.
  • Explain the council route, needs assessment and financial assessment briefly, and link to the NHS rather than giving advice.
  • Set out your real process from first call to reviews, and only promise what you deliver.
  • Include prices or how to get a quote, out-of-hours contact, complaints and your CQC rating.

When a family first looks for home care, they rarely know where to start. Who arranges it, the council or an agency? Will the council pay? What happens at the first visit? A clear "how home care works" page on your agency's website answers those questions in plain English and walks families from first call to first visit. It builds trust before anyone picks up the phone, and it saves your office from explaining the same steps on every enquiry call. This guide covers what the NHS and CQC tell families, which questions your page should answer, and how to lay it out. It's written for domiciliary care agencies in England.

What families are told before they find you

Many families will have read the NHS guide to help at home from a paid carer before they reach your website. It's worth knowing what it says, because your page should fit with it rather than contradict it.

The NHS page explains that:

  • homecare (also called domiciliary care or home help) can be temporary, such as a few weeks after an illness, or long term
  • it's flexible, from an hour a week to several hours a day, at night, or live-in care
  • the local council can arrange homecare if someone is eligible, or families can arrange their own
  • all homecare agencies must register with the CQC, and families can read an agency's latest inspection report on the CQC website
  • if families pay for themselves, the agency should be able to give a clear price list

It also gives families a list of questions to ask an agency before using them. Those questions make an excellent outline for your page.

The questions your how home care works page should answer

The NHS suggests families ask an agency:

  • what charges they'll be expected to pay
  • what services are charged as extras
  • whether your carers have looked after someone with similar needs
  • how you'll choose the most suitable carer
  • whether carers will visit in a specific time slot, and tell the family if they're delayed
  • what training your carers get
  • whether you have a standard contract they can read before signing, if they're paying for themselves
  • if the council is contributing, whether they can see the contract the council signed with you
  • how to contact you during the day, in an emergency or outside office hours

If your page answers each of these clearly, families arrive at the first call already reassured, and often ready to book an assessment.

Explaining the routes into care

Families often don't know whether to call you or the council. Explain both routes simply, and link to the official guidance rather than giving your own interpretation.

Through the council

The NHS guide to getting a care needs assessment explains that the first step for council help is a needs assessment from social services. It says the assessment is free and anyone can ask for one. Someone from the council, such as a social worker or occupational therapist, asks how the person manages everyday tasks like washing, dressing and cooking.

The NHS homecare page adds that even families arranging care themselves may find a needs assessment useful, because it helps explain to an agency what kind of help is needed.

Paying for care

The NHS page on the financial assessment (means test) explains that, if the needs assessment finds someone needs help, a financial assessment works out whether the council will pay towards it. It says that in England the council generally helps with care costs if someone has savings of less than £23,250, and that if a paid carer comes into someone's home, the value of their house isn't included in the financial assessment.

If the council does contribute, the person is offered a personal budget, which they can take as a direct payment or have the council arrange their care.

On your page, explain these steps briefly, link to the NHS pages and tell families to check their own situation with their council. Thresholds and rules change, so don't quote figures you won't keep updated, and don't give financial advice.

Paying privately

For families arranging care themselves, explain your own process and prices clearly. Say whether you accept clients funded by direct payments, and whether you work with the local council.

Your process, step by step

This is the heart of the page. Set out what happens from first contact to ongoing care, in the order it really happens at your agency. A typical structure:

Step What happens What to tell families
1. First call A conversation about needs and timescales Who they'll speak to and when you're open
2. Assessment visit Meeting the person at home Whether there's a charge, how long it takes, who should be there
3. Care plan A written plan of the care and support That the person and family are involved
4. Choosing carers Matching carers to needs and preferences How you match, and whether they meet carers first
5. First visits Care starts What to expect on the first day
6. Reviews Regular reviews of the care plan How often, and how to ask for changes

Only include steps you actually follow. If you don't charge for an assessment visit, say so; if you do, say how much.

Use what the CQC says good care looks like

The CQC's page on what you can expect from a good home-care agency gives families a picture of good care. Among other things, it says that:

  • people are introduced to staff who will provide their care
  • if the person caring for them needs to change at short notice, they're told who to expect
  • care is set out in a written plan, and the person, and their family where appropriate, are involved in developing it
  • the plan changes as needs and preferences change
  • concerns and complaints are taken seriously, investigated and responded to in good time

Where your process does these things, say so in your own words, explaining how you do them. Don't claim more than you deliver: families will hold you to it, and so will inspectors.

Practical details families want

The NHS also lists what families should expect from agency carers, including that they bring an identity card, announce their arrival before coming in, keep entry codes confidential, and know what to do if they can't get in or the person has had an accident. A short "What to expect from our carers" section that covers these points, in your own words, is reassuring.

Also include:

  • Areas covered, with a link to your area pages
  • Hours and out-of-hours contact, including an emergency number if you have one
  • How billing works, such as monthly invoices, and how to pay
  • How to raise a concern or complaint, with a link to your complaints procedure
  • Your CQC rating, displayed as the CQC requires

Before and after: the opening of a how it works page

Here's an invented example.

Before: "We provide high-quality, person-centred care tailored to your needs. Contact us today to find out more."

After: "Arranging care for someone you love can feel overwhelming. Here's exactly what happens when you contact us: a friendly call with our care coordinator, a free home visit within a few days to talk through what's needed, a written care plan you help to shape, and an introduction to your carers before care begins. If you're not sure whether the council can help with costs, we explain how needs assessments work below."

Replace every detail with your own. Don't promise timescales or free visits you don't offer.

Common mistakes

  • Jargon. Families may not know "domiciliary", "package of care" or "direct payments". Explain terms the first time you use them.
  • Hiding prices. The NHS says agencies should be able to give self-funding clients a clear price list. If you can't publish exact prices, explain what affects them and how to get a quote.
  • Giving financial advice. Explain the process, link to the NHS and your council, and leave individual advice to them.
  • Promising what you can't deliver. "Same carer every time" is a promise many agencies can't keep. Say what you aim for and how you handle changes.
  • No clear next step. End every section with a way to call, book an assessment or send an enquiry.

Frequently asked questions

What should a how home care works page include?

Explain what homecare is, the routes into care through the council or privately, how funding is assessed, your process from first call to care plan and reviews, how you match carers, what to expect from your carers, prices or how to get a quote, out-of-hours contact, how to complain and your CQC rating.

Should a home care agency explain council funding on its website?

A short explanation helps families, as long as you link to official guidance. The NHS explains that a free needs assessment comes first, followed by a financial assessment to see whether the council will pay towards care. Tell families to check their situation with their council, and don't give individual financial advice.

Should home care agencies publish their prices?

The NHS tells families that an agency should be able to give self-funding clients a clear price list. If you can't publish exact prices because they depend on the care needed, explain what affects the price, whether the assessment visit is free and how families can get a written quote.

What questions do families ask a home care agency?

The NHS suggests families ask about charges and extras, carers' experience and training, how carers are chosen, visit time slots and late arrivals, contracts, and how to contact the agency during the day, in an emergency or out of hours. Answering these on your website reassures families before they call.
