Engineering Firm About Us Page: Show Clients Your Engineers

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

An engineering firm about us page should introduce each engineer by name with a real photo, their role, current qualifications and registrations, experience and specialisms, and links to their projects. Explain who clients deal with and who signs off designs, list only current memberships, and link to your enquiry form.

Key takeaways

  • Clients want to know who will check and sign off their design, so name your engineers.
  • Google's helpful content guidance asks whether it's clear who created content and points to About pages as trust signals.
  • Show professional titles exactly as each engineer holds them; UK titles can be checked on the Engineering Council's RegCheck.
  • Never use stock photos, made-up people or lapsed credentials.
  • Link engineer profiles to projects, article bylines, area pages and your enquiry form.

When a builder, architect or homeowner is choosing a structural or civil engineer, they want to know who will actually look at their drawings and sign off their design. Your engineering firm's about us page is where they find out. On many consultancy websites it's a short paragraph about "a commitment to excellence" and a stock photo of a hard hat. That tells a client nothing. A good about us page introduces your engineers, shows their qualifications and experience, and makes it easy for clients to check credentials and get in touch. This guide covers what to include, what Google says about showing who's behind your content, and how to lay out the page. It's written for consultancies in Australia and the UK.

Why the about us page matters for engineering firms

Clients aren't buying a product; they're trusting a person's judgement with a building. Before they send you their drawings, many will look at who you are.

Google's guidance on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content points the same way. It asks whether content presents information in a way that makes you want to trust it, including "background about the author or the site that publishes it, such as through links to an author page or a site's About page". It also asks: "Is it self-evident to your visitors who authored your content?"

Google says its systems give even more weight to content with strong signs of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness for topics that could significantly affect people's health, finances or safety. Structural advice can affect safety, so showing the real people behind it is worth the effort.

What to put on your engineering firm about us page

A short, specific introduction

Two or three sentences on what the firm does, for whom and where: "We're a structural and civil engineering consultancy in [region], working mainly on residential extensions, small commercial projects and retaining walls for builders, architects and homeowners." Skip the mission statement.

Your engineers, one by one

This is the heart of the page. For each engineer, include:

  • Name and photo. A real, recent photo of the person, not a stock image.
  • Role. Director, senior engineer, graduate engineer, drafter.
  • Qualifications and registrations. Degrees, chartered status, and any registration that applies where you work.
  • Experience. Years in practice and the types of projects they specialise in.
  • Projects. Links to two or three projects they worked on, with client permission.
  • A personal line, if they want one. Optional, but it helps people connect.

Ask each person what they're comfortable sharing, and keep the information current when people join, leave or gain new qualifications.

How the team works together

Explain who a client deals with: "Every project has a named engineer from first call to final certificate." If graduates work under supervision, say how designs are checked and who signs them off. Clients find this reassuring.

Memberships and registrations

List the professional bodies the firm and its engineers belong to, and any schemes your work is registered under. Only list memberships that are current.

A clear next step

End with a link to your fee proposal form or contact page. My guide to the structural engineer enquiry form covers what that form should ask.

Showing qualifications honestly

Professional titles are a big part of an engineer's credibility, and they're also protected.

In the UK, the Engineering Council's RegCheck lets employers, clients and members of the public confirm whether an engineer or technician is registered with the Engineering Council and entitled to use one of its protected professional titles, such as Chartered Engineer (CEng), Incorporated Engineer (IEng) or Engineering Technician (EngTech). It only shows registrations that are currently active.

That has two practical consequences for your website:

  • Use titles exactly as each engineer holds them. If someone is working towards chartership, say "working towards CEng", not "CEng".
  • Make checking easy. Use each engineer's full registered name on their profile, so a client can check them on RegCheck or the relevant institution's register.

In Australia, engineering registration requirements differ between states and territories. Check the current rules with the registration body in each state you work in, and show each engineer's registrations accurately.

Google's guidance is blunt about the alternative: it says fabricating creator profiles, "such as by using AI-generated headshots, made-up names, or false credentials", is a form of deception that makes a page untrustworthy to both users and its systems. Never pad a team page with people who don't work for you.

Linking people to their work

An about us page works best when it's connected to the rest of your site.

  • Link engineer profiles to their projects, and add "Engineer: [name]" to project pages. My guide to engineering project portfolios covers how to write those.
  • Add bylines to articles and guides, linking to the author's profile, as Google's guidance encourages where readers might expect one.
  • Link area pages to the local team, so a client in a particular area sees who would visit them.
  • Link the careers page to the team page. Prospective engineers want to know who they'd work with too; my guide to recruiting structural engineers covers that side.

Before and after: an engineer profile

Here's an invented example.

Before: "John has extensive experience in structural engineering and is passionate about delivering excellent outcomes for clients."

After: "John Smith BEng CEng MIStructE, Director. John has designed residential and light commercial structures for 15 years, mainly steel and timber alterations to older homes. Recent projects: a two-storey rear extension in [suburb] and a basement conversion in [town]. John reviews and signs off all residential designs."

The details here are made up. Replace them with each engineer's real, current information, and check every title and membership before publishing.

Common mistakes

  • Stock photos. Clients can usually tell, and it undermines the trust the page is meant to build.
  • Out-of-date profiles. An engineer who left two years ago still listed as a director raises questions.
  • Inflated titles. Listing a title someone doesn't hold, or a lapsed membership, can mislead clients and may breach the rules on protected titles.
  • No individual names. "Our experienced team" with no names gives clients nobody to trust.
  • A dead end. An about us page with no link to projects, services or the enquiry form wastes the visitor's interest.

Frequently asked questions

What should a structural engineering firm include on its about us page?

Include a short, specific introduction, then a profile for each engineer with their name, real photo, role, current qualifications and registrations, experience, specialisms and links to projects. Explain who clients deal with and who signs off designs, list current memberships, and end with a link to your fee proposal or enquiry form.

Does an about us page help SEO?

Google's guidance on helpful content asks whether it's clear who created your content and mentions background about the author or site, such as an About page, as something that builds trust. A detailed team page linked to projects and article bylines supports that, alongside the rest of your site's content.

How can clients check an engineer's chartered status in the UK?

The Engineering Council's RegCheck tool lets clients confirm whether an engineer or technician currently holds an Engineering Council title such as CEng, IEng or EngTech. It needs an exact match on the person's details, so use each engineer's full registered name on your website.

Should graduate engineers appear on the team page?

Yes, if they're happy to. Show their role accurately, such as graduate engineer or working towards chartership, and explain how their designs are checked and who signs them off. That's honest, and it reassures clients that every design is reviewed by an experienced engineer.
