Home Care Pricing Page: Show Families What Care Costs

Hamid Ali AsadNovember 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

A home care pricing page tells families what care costs: hourly rates by time and day, minimum visit lengths, any extras, what affects the price, how billing works and how council funding fits in, with links to NHS guidance. Headline prices should include non-optional fees, and every page should end with a clear way to get a quote.

Key takeaways

  • The NHS tells self-funding families an agency should be able to give a clear price list.
  • Publish rates, a 'from' price with clear explanation, or a clear quote process, whichever is honest for your agency.
  • List every extra; the NHS suggests families ask what's charged as extras.
  • The CAP Code says quoted prices must include non-optional fees and 'from' prices must not mislead.
  • Explain council funding briefly and link to the NHS rather than giving financial advice.

"How much does it cost?" is one of the first questions a family asks when they start looking for home care, and many agency websites don't answer it. Families then either call several agencies to compare, or move on to one that does show its prices. A clear home care pricing page answers the question honestly, explains what affects the cost, and shows how council funding fits in. It saves your office time on calls and brings you enquiries from families who already know roughly what to expect. This guide covers what to include, what the NHS tells families about prices, the UK advertising rules on price claims, and how to lay the page out. It's written for domiciliary care agencies in England.

What families are told about prices

The NHS guide to help at home from a paid carer, which many families read before they find you, says that if someone is paying for their own care, an agency "should be able to give you a clear price list" and will send a monthly bill. It also suggests families ask an agency what charges they'll be expected to pay and what services are charged as extras.

So families arrive expecting a clear answer. A pricing page that gives one, or explains exactly how to get one, meets that expectation instead of frustrating it.

Should you publish exact prices?

You have three realistic options:

Approach What it looks like Works well when
Full price list Hourly rates by time of day and day of week, plus any extras Your rates are the same for most clients
Starting prices with clear explanation "From" prices with a list of what changes the cost Prices vary with care needs or area
No prices, with a clear quote process What affects the price and how quickly you'll quote Every package is genuinely bespoke

Many agencies can at least publish standard hourly rates and their weekend and bank holiday rates. Whichever you choose, families should come away knowing roughly what care will cost and what happens next.

What to include on a home care pricing page

Your rates and what they cover

If you publish rates, set them out clearly:

  • standard hourly rate for weekday visits
  • evening, night, weekend and bank holiday rates, if different
  • any minimum visit length, such as 30 or 60 minutes
  • how shorter visits are charged
  • live-in or overnight care rates, if you offer them

Anything charged as an extra

The NHS suggests families ask what's charged as extras, so answer it before they ask. Mileage when carers drive clients to appointments, assessment visits, set-up fees, key safes or anything else outside the hourly rate should be listed here.

What affects the price

If prices vary, explain why: the time of day, the length and number of visits, two-carer visits, specialist care such as dementia or complex needs, and the area you're travelling to.

How billing works

Say how often you invoice, how families pay, when payment is due and what notice is needed to change or cancel visits.

How council funding fits in

Many families don't realise they may get help with costs. Explain briefly, and link to official guidance. The NHS page on the financial assessment (means test) explains that, after a needs assessment, the council carries out a financial assessment to see whether it will pay towards care, and that if a paid carer comes into someone's home, the value of their house isn't included. If the council contributes, the person can choose a direct payment or have the council arrange their care.

On your page, say whether you work with the local council and whether you accept clients who pay with direct payments. Tell families to check their own situation with their council rather than relying on your summary, and don't give financial advice.

A clear next step

End with how to get a written quote, how long it takes, and whether the assessment visit is free.

The UK rules on price claims

The CAP Code's rules on misleading advertising apply to prices on your website. Among other things, they say:

  • Quoted prices must include non-optional taxes, duties, fees and charges that apply to all or most buyers
  • If a charge can't be calculated in advance, for example because it depends on the client's circumstances, you must make clear it's excluded from the advertised price and say how it's calculated
  • "From" and "up to" prices must not mislead by exaggerating how available the advertised price really is
  • Price statements must not mislead by omission, undue emphasis or distortion

In practice:

  • if every client pays a set-up fee, don't leave it out of the headline price
  • only say "from £X an hour" if a meaningful number of clients actually pay that
  • show weekend and bank holiday rates as clearly as weekday ones
  • keep the page up to date when your prices change

This isn't legal advice. Check the current rules and take advice if you're unsure.

Laying out the page

  1. Start with a short, honest introduction. "Here's what our care costs, what's included and how to get a personalised quote."
  2. Show the rates in a simple table. Weekday, evening, weekend and bank holiday rates, with minimum visit lengths.
  3. List extras and what affects the price.
  4. Explain billing and notice periods.
  5. Explain council funding briefly, with links to the NHS.
  6. Add a short FAQ for questions families ask on the phone.
  7. End with a quote request form and phone number.

Link the pricing page from your main navigation, your "how home care works" page and your service pages. My guide to the how home care works page covers the process side.

Before and after: a pricing section

Here's an invented example.

Before: "Our prices are competitive. Please call us for a quote."

After: "Weekday visits: £X per hour. Evenings and weekends: £Y per hour. Bank holidays: £Z per hour. Minimum visit: 30 minutes. No set-up fee. Mileage for trips out is charged at [rate]. We invoice monthly in arrears. Your first assessment visit is free, and we'll send a written quote within two working days."

Replace every figure and promise with your own. Don't publish prices or timescales you can't honour.

Common mistakes

  • "Competitive prices" with no numbers. Families can't compare, so many move on.
  • Hidden extras. Mileage or set-up fees that appear only on the first invoice damage trust.
  • Unrealistic "from" prices. A rate few clients actually pay can mislead.
  • Out-of-date prices. Update the page whenever your rates change.
  • Giving funding advice. Explain the process and link to the NHS and your council.

Frequently asked questions

Should a home care agency publish its prices online?

The NHS tells families that, if they're paying for their own care, an agency should be able to give a clear price list. Publishing standard hourly rates, or at least explaining what affects the price and how to get a quote quickly, meets that expectation and saves your office from answering the same question on every call.

What should a home care pricing page include?

Include hourly rates by time of day and day of week, minimum visit lengths, any extras such as mileage or set-up fees, what affects the price, how billing and notice periods work, how council funding and direct payments fit in, and a clear way to request a written quote.

Can I use 'from' prices for home care?

Yes, but the CAP Code says 'from' prices must not mislead by exaggerating how available the advertised price is. Only use a 'from' price that a meaningful number of clients actually pay, include any non-optional fees, and explain clearly what makes the price higher for other clients.

Should my pricing page explain council funding?

A short explanation helps families. The NHS explains that a needs assessment comes first, then a financial assessment to see if the council will pay towards care. Say whether you work with the council and accept direct payments, link to the NHS pages, and tell families to check their own situation with their council.
