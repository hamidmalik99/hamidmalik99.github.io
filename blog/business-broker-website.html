Business Broker Website: How to Win More Seller Listings

Hamid Ali AsadOctober 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A business broker website that wins seller listings gives owners their own path, separate from buyers. It explains the selling process, offers a valuation request, shows sector experience and fees, and proves confidentiality with blind listings and NDA-gated details. Testimonials must be genuine under the FTC rule, and private pages should use noindex.

Key takeaways

  • Sellers need a separate journey from buyers, with process, valuation, fees and track record explained.
  • Blind listings and NDA-gated details show confidentiality in practice rather than just promising it.
  • Use noindex, not robots.txt, for reachable pages that shouldn't appear in Google.
  • Under the FTC rule, testimonials on your own site are your responsibility, and incentives tied to positive sentiment are a problem.
  • Your Google Business Profile name must match your real business name, without added keywords.

Most business broker websites are built for buyers. The homepage is a grid of listings, and the "Sell your business" page is an afterthought. Yet a brokerage's growth usually depends on winning more sell-side mandates, not more buyer traffic. A business broker website that wins seller listings has to do something harder: persuade an owner who is nervous, private and often unsure whether now is the time, to have a confidential conversation with you. This guide covers the pages, forms and confidentiality features that make that happen, plus the rules on testimonials and Google listings that brokers need to get right.

Why sellers and buyers need different journeys

Buyers arrive wanting to browse. Sellers arrive wanting reassurance. An owner thinking about selling usually wants answers to a few questions before they'll talk to anyone:

  • Will this stay confidential? Will my staff, customers or competitors find out?
  • What is my business likely to be worth, and how is that worked out?
  • What does the process look like, how long does it take, and what will it cost me?
  • Has this broker sold businesses like mine?

If your site sends sellers into the same listings grid as buyers, none of those questions get answered. Give sellers their own clear path from the homepage: a prominent "Selling your business" section with its own pages and its own call to action.

Pages a business broker website needs for sellers

A "how selling works" page

Lay out your process step by step: first confidential conversation, valuation, preparing the information memorandum, marketing to buyers, screening and NDAs, offers, due diligence and closing. Say roughly how long each stage typically takes at your firm and what the owner needs to do at each step. Owners who understand the process are more likely to start it.

A valuation request page

A "What's my business worth?" page works well as a seller lead generator because it matches the question owners are already asking. Explain how you approach valuation in plain English. Be clear about what the owner gets: an indicative range, a written opinion of value or a formal valuation. Never promise a number before you've seen the figures.

Sector and deal-size pages

If you specialize, say so. Pages for the sectors you know well (healthcare practices, home services, manufacturing, e-commerce, professional services) and the deal sizes you handle help owners see themselves in your work. They also give Google specific pages to rank for searches like "sell my HVAC business" or "business broker for dental practices".

Fees and engagement terms, explained

You don't have to publish your fee schedule, but explain the model: success fees, any retainer or valuation fee, and the typical length of an engagement. Owners compare brokers, and silence on fees reads as a warning sign.

Your team and track record, described honestly

Show who will actually handle the sale, with real photos, backgrounds and any professional credentials. If you list closed deals, describe them accurately and only with the client's permission, for example "Sold: commercial cleaning business, Midwest" rather than naming a client who expected discretion.

Confidentiality features that build trust

Confidentiality is a major worry for sellers, so your website should show it in how it works, not just say it.

Blind listings

Public listing pages should describe the business without identifying it: sector, broad region, revenue and cash-flow ranges, and the reason for sale, but no name, exact address or photos that give it away. Review every teaser with the seller before it goes live.

NDA-gated details

Put the information memorandum and identifying details behind an NDA step. A buyer requests access, signs electronically and is screened before seeing anything sensitive. This protects the seller and gives you a record of who has seen what.

Keep private pages out of Google

Pages that sit behind a login generally can't be crawled. But some pages may be reachable without logging in, such as "thank you" pages, NDA confirmation pages or old listings. For those, Google's documentation on blocking indexing with noindex explains that a noindex meta tag or HTTP header tells Google to drop the page from its results. It also warns of a common mistake: if you block the page in robots.txt instead, Google can't see the noindex rule, and the page can still appear in results if other pages link to it. Most WordPress SEO plugins let you set noindex page by page without touching code.

Testimonials and reviews: the FTC rule

Seller testimonials are powerful, but in the US they now come with clear rules. The FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule took effect on October 21, 2024. It addresses deceptive and unfair conduct involving consumer reviews and testimonials, and courts can impose civil penalties for knowing violations.

A few points from the FTC staff's questions and answers matter for brokers:

  • Testimonials on your own site are your responsibility. The FTC says a business that puts testimonials on its own website is disseminating them, not just "hosting" them. If those testimonials are fake or false, the business could be liable.
  • Incentives tied to sentiment are a problem. The rule covers compensation or incentives conditioned on reviews expressing a particular sentiment. Asking every client for honest feedback is fine; rewarding only positive reviews is not.
  • Insiders need care. The rule includes disclosure requirements for certain situations involving company insiders, so staff or partners shouldn't post reviews of your firm without making the relationship clear.

The FTC notes its staff guidance isn't definitive, so check the current guidance and take legal advice if you're unsure. This isn't legal advice.

Local SEO for business brokers

Many owners search locally: "business broker near me" or "business broker" plus their city. Your Google Business Profile matters here. Google's guidelines for representing your business say your profile name should match your real-world business name, and that adding extra keywords or service information to it isn't permitted and could get the profile suspended. If you meet clients at your office, show the address. If you don't receive visitors, set a service area and hide the address instead.

On your own site, back this up with a page for each main area you serve, with genuine local content: the types of businesses you've sold there and the local market you know.

Turning visits into seller conversations

A seller inquiry form should be short and reassuring. Ask for their name, a contact method, the type of business, an approximate revenue band and a rough timeline. State clearly that the inquiry is confidential and who will see it. Offer a phone number and a booking link too, because many owners prefer to talk first.

Frequently asked questions

How do business brokers get more listings?

Referrals from accountants, attorneys and past clients remain important, but your website should also convert owners who look you up. Give sellers their own section explaining the process, a valuation request page, sector pages, your fee model and anonymized closed deals, with a short confidential inquiry form and a phone number.

What should a business broker website include?

Separate paths for sellers and buyers, a step-by-step selling process, a valuation request page, sector and deal-size pages, an explanation of fees, your team and permissioned track record, blind listings with NDA-gated details, and an accurate Google Business Profile backed by genuine local area pages.

How do brokers keep business listings confidential online?

Publish blind teasers that describe the sector, broad region and financial ranges without identifying the business. Put the information memorandum and identifying details behind an NDA step with buyer screening. For any reachable page that shouldn't appear in Google, use a noindex tag rather than blocking it in robots.txt.

Can business brokers use client testimonials on their website?

Yes, if they're genuine. The FTC says a business that puts testimonials on its own website is disseminating them and could be liable if they're fake or false. Don't offer incentives conditioned on positive sentiment, and make insider relationships clear. Check the FTC's current guidance and get legal advice if unsure.
