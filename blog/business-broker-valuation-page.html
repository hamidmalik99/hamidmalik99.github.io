Business Broker Valuation Page: Turn "What's It Worth?" Into Listings

Hamid Ali AsadOctober 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A business broker valuation page should explain in plain English how businesses are valued, what drives value up and down, and exactly what the owner gets when they ask. Use a short, confidential form with revenue and profit bands, label any calculator as an indicative range, and make only claims you can back up.

Key takeaways

  • A valuation page answers the question owners ask first and starts a relationship before they choose a broker.
  • Explain how value is worked out and what you offer, rather than promising a precise number online.
  • If you use a calculator, show a labeled range, explain its limits and route the owner to a person.
  • The FTC requires a reasonable basis for ad claims, and the CAP Code requires evidence for objective claims before publishing.
  • In the UK, only call a valuation free if the owner pays nothing beyond the unavoidable cost of responding.

"What's my business worth?" is often the first question an owner has when they start thinking about selling. It may be months or years before they're ready to list. A business broker valuation page answers that question in a way that starts a relationship with you, rather than sending the owner to a free online calculator that spits out a number and forgets them. This guide covers what to put on a valuation page, whether to use a calculator, what to ask on the form, and the US and UK advertising rules that apply to claims about value.

Why a valuation page is a seller lead source

Most owners aren't ready to talk about listing the first time they visit your site. They are, however, curious about value. A page built around that question:

  • matches what they're already searching for
  • gives you a reason to talk before they've chosen a broker
  • lets you start a conversation months before a listing, when you can help them prepare

The goal isn't to give a precise number online. It's to explain how value is worked out, set realistic expectations and make it easy to ask you for a proper, confidential opinion.

What to put on a business broker valuation page

Explain how businesses are valued, in plain English

Owners often have a number in their head that's based on revenue, a friend's sale or a headline. Explain the basics of how your firm approaches value: earnings and cash flow, how adjustments for owner's pay and one-off costs work, why recurring revenue and customer concentration matter, and how the market for that kind of business affects the price. You don't need formulas; you need to show you know what you're doing and that value depends on detail.

Say what drives value up and down

A short list helps owners see what to work on: clean financial records, a business that runs without the owner, diversified customers, documented processes, contracts that transfer. This makes the page useful even for owners who aren't selling yet, and it naturally leads to "talk to us about preparing your business".

Be clear about what you offer

Spell out what the owner gets when they ask:

  • an indicative range after a short call
  • a written opinion of value
  • a formal valuation, and whether that's a separate paid service

Say whether there's a fee and what's included. Vague offers create suspicion.

Show relevant experience

Sectors and deal sizes you work with, and anonymized examples of businesses you've sold, used with permission. If you show a closed deal, describe it accurately.

Make the next step confidential and easy

A short form or a booking link, with a clear statement about confidentiality and who will see the information.

Should you use an online valuation calculator?

Calculators can attract visitors and capture contact details, but they come with risks. An automated number can anchor an owner on an unrealistic figure before you've seen the books. And if the tool presents its output as more reliable than it is, you may have an advertising problem (see below).

If you use one:

  • Show a range, not a single figure.
  • Label it clearly as an indicative estimate based only on the figures entered, not a valuation.
  • Explain the main limitations next to the result: it doesn't account for the quality of earnings, customer concentration, industry conditions or deal terms.
  • Route to a human. The result should lead straight to "book a confidential call to discuss your business".

A simpler, lower-risk alternative is a short form that asks for a few figures and promises a personal response within a set time.

What to ask on the valuation request form

Keep it short and ask only what you need for a first conversation.

Field Why it helps
Name, email, phone To reply
Type of business and location Sector and market matter to value
Approximate annual revenue (band) Sizes the opportunity
Approximate profit or owner's earnings (band) The starting point for most valuations
Years trading Track record
Reason for asking (selling soon, planning, curious) Sets the tone of your reply
Preferred contact method and time Respects privacy and schedules

Use bands rather than exact figures in the first step. Owners may be more comfortable sharing ranges before they know you, and you'll get the detail later.

Don't over-collect

The UK ICO's guidance on data minimisation says personal data should be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for your purpose, and that you must not collect personal data on the off-chance it might be useful in the future. Even if you work only in the US, it's a sensible standard for a form that asks owners about their finances. Link to your privacy policy next to the form.

Advertising rules: what you can claim about value

Valuation pages often make claims: "free valuation", "we get owners the best price", "our clients sell for more". These are advertising claims, and both the US and UK have rules about them.

United States

The FTC's Advertising FAQs for small business set out the basic principles under the FTC Act: advertising must be truthful and non-deceptive, advertisers must have evidence to back up their claims, and ads cannot be unfair. The FAQ explains that before an ad runs, a company has to have a "reasonable basis" for its claims, meaning objective evidence. It also notes that statements from satisfied customers usually aren't enough to support a claim that requires objective evaluation.

So a claim like "our sellers achieve higher prices than average" needs real evidence behind it. If you can't support a comparative or numerical claim, don't make it.

United Kingdom

The CAP Code's section on misleading advertising says marketing communications must not materially mislead. Marketers must hold documentary evidence to prove claims consumers are likely to regard as objective before publishing. The section also covers qualifications: marketing must not mislead by omitting significant limitations and qualifications, and qualifications must be presented clearly. That's directly relevant to calculator results and "free valuation" offers.

The same section has rules on the word "free": it must not be used if the consumer has to pay anything other than the unavoidable cost of responding. If your "free valuation" is only free when the owner lists with you, or carries other conditions, make that clear or don't call it free.

Both regulators' guidance depends on the facts, so check the current rules and take legal advice if you're unsure. This isn't legal advice.

A before and after example

Here's an invented example of a valuation page headline and offer.

Before: "FREE instant business valuation! Find out what your business is REALLY worth in 60 seconds. We get our clients top dollar."

After: "What's your business worth? Get a confidential, indicative range from an experienced broker. Share a few figures and we'll call you within two working days to talk through how buyers would see your business. There's no charge for this conversation and no obligation to list. A formal written valuation is available as a separate service."

The second version sets accurate expectations, avoids unsupported claims and gives the owner a reason to talk to a person.

Frequently asked questions

Should a business broker website have a valuation calculator?

It can, if it's handled carefully. Show a range rather than one number, label it as an indicative estimate based only on the figures entered, explain the main limitations next to the result, and route the owner to a confidential call. A short request form with a personal reply is a simpler, lower-risk alternative.

What should a business valuation request form ask?

Keep it short: name and contact details, type of business and location, approximate annual revenue and profit as bands, years trading, the reason for asking and the preferred contact method. Use bands rather than exact figures at first, link to your privacy policy and collect the detail later in the conversation.

Can a business broker advertise a free valuation?

In the UK, the CAP Code says marketing must not describe something as free if the consumer has to pay anything other than the unavoidable cost of responding, so conditions such as listing with you must be made clear. In the US, the FTC requires advertising to be truthful and backed by evidence. Check current guidance and take legal advice.

What claims can business brokers make about sale prices?

Only claims you can prove. The FTC says advertisers need a reasonable basis, meaning objective evidence, before an ad runs, and customer testimonials usually aren't enough for claims needing objective evaluation. The UK CAP Code requires documentary evidence for objective claims before publishing. Avoid comparative or numerical claims you can't support.
