Fractional CFO Case Studies: How to Write Them for Your Website

Hamid Ali AsadOctober 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A strong fractional CFO case study describes the client by industry and stage, states the problem in plain language, explains the specific work and time frame, and reports outcomes you can back up. Get written permission for names and quotes, label illustrative examples clearly, disclose unexpected connections and don't imply one client's results are typical.

Key takeaways

  • Specific case studies differentiate a fractional CFO website far more than generic service taglines.
  • Use the same five parts every time: client, problem, what you did, what changed and an approved quote.
  • Anonymized case studies are fine; presenting a made-up story as a real client is not.
  • The FTC says endorsements must be honest, and results must not be implied as typical without support.
  • Testimonials on your own website are your responsibility under the FTC's reviews and testimonials rule.

Most fractional CFO websites say the same things: "strategic financial leadership", "scale with confidence", "CFO-level insight without the full-time cost". Founders read a dozen of these before they book a call, and the sites blur together. Fractional CFO case studies are what separate you. A specific story about a business like theirs, with a clear problem, what you did and what changed, does more than any tagline. This guide covers how to write case studies for your fractional CFO website, how to handle client confidentiality, and the US rules on testimonials and results claims you need to respect.

Why case studies matter more than taglines

A founder hiring a fractional CFO is trusting you with their numbers, their board and often their fundraising. They want evidence that you've dealt with situations like theirs. Generic service pages can't give them that; a good case study can.

Case studies also help you get found. A page about "preparing a SaaS business for its Series A" or "fixing cash flow at a growing manufacturer" matches the specific problems owners search for, and gives AI search tools concrete material to cite. A simple structure that works

Keep every case study to the same five parts so readers can scan them quickly.

1. The client, described the way they'd describe themselves

Industry, size or stage, and location if relevant: "a 40-person B2B software company preparing to raise", "a family-owned distributor with two warehouses". If you can't name the client, this line does the work of making the story recognizable.

2. The problem in their words

What was keeping the founder up at night? Month-end close taking weeks, no reliable cash forecast, a board asking questions nobody could answer, an investor data room that didn't exist. Use the language a founder would use, not finance jargon.

3. What you did

The specific work: built a 13-week cash flow forecast, rebuilt the chart of accounts, introduced monthly board packs, prepared the financial model and data room, renegotiated payment terms. Mention the time frame and how many days a month you worked with them. This shows prospects what an engagement with you actually looks like.

4. What changed

Outcomes, described accurately. Faster month-end close, a forecast the board trusts, a completed raise, an exit process that ran to timetable. If you use numbers, use real ones from the engagement and be ready to back them up. See the rules below before you publish any figures.

5. A quote, if the client agrees

A short, honest quote from the founder adds a human voice. Get written permission for the wording and for how they're identified.

Confidentiality: named, anonymized or composite?

Finance work is sensitive, so many clients won't want to be named. You have three honest options:

Approach What it looks like When to use it
Named Company name, founder name and quote, with written permission Clients happy to be public, often after an exit or raise
Anonymized "A Series A SaaS company in Austin", details changed only where they'd identify the client Most engagements
Clearly labeled example "Here's how an engagement typically runs", not presented as a real client When you have no permissioned stories yet

What you must not do is present a made-up story as a real client engagement. If an example is illustrative, say so.

The US rules on testimonials and results

The FTC's rules on endorsements and reviews apply to how you present client stories and quotes on your own website.

Endorsements must be honest and typical results matter

The FTC's Endorsement Guides Q&A says endorsements must be honest and not misleading, and must reflect the honest opinion of the endorser. An endorsement can't be used to make a claim the marketer couldn't legally make directly.

The same guidance explains that if an advertiser doesn't have proof that an endorser's experience represents what people will generally achieve, an ad featuring that endorser must make clear what the generally expected results are. For a fractional CFO, that means a headline result from one standout client shouldn't be presented as what every client can expect. Add context, such as the starting point and the work involved, and avoid implying it's typical unless you can support that.

Disclose connections readers wouldn't expect

The Endorsement Guides Q&A also says that if there's a connection between an endorser and the business that readers wouldn't expect, and it would affect how they view the endorsement, it should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. Its examples include an endorser who is a relative or employee, or who has been paid or given something of value. If a "client testimonial" comes from a friend, a referral partner or someone who received a discount for it, say so.

Testimonials on your site are your responsibility

The FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule Q&A says a business that puts testimonials on its own website is disseminating them, not merely hosting them, and could be liable if they're fake or false.

The FTC notes that its staff guidance isn't definitive and depends on the facts. Check the current guidance and take legal advice if you're unsure. This isn't legal advice.

Before and after: a case study summary

Here's an invented example of the same engagement written two ways.

Before: "We partnered with an exciting tech company to transform their finance function and unlock growth. The results were amazing!"

After: "Client: a 35-person B2B software company preparing for its first institutional raise. Problem: management accounts took six weeks, and there was no forecast investors could rely on. What we did: two days a month for nine months; rebuilt the month-end process, introduced a rolling 13-week cash forecast and a three-statement model, and prepared the data room. What changed: month-end now closes within the second week, and the founder used the model and data room throughout the raise. Note: results depend on each business's starting point."

The second version is specific, verifiable and honest about context. It's also more persuasive.

Where case studies should live on your site

  • A case studies index, filterable by industry and service, linked from your main navigation.
  • One page per case study, so each can rank for its own problem and be shared on its own.
  • Short excerpts on service pages. Put the cash-flow case study on your cash-flow forecasting page, and the fundraising story on your fundraising support page.
  • A strong call to action at the end: "Facing something similar? Book a 30-minute call."

Frequently asked questions

What should a fractional CFO case study include?

Describe the client by industry, size and stage; the problem in the founder's words; the specific work you did, the time frame and how many days a month; the outcomes, described accurately with context; and, with written permission, a short quote. Keep the same structure across every case study so prospects can scan and compare them quickly.

Can I publish case studies without naming the client?

Yes. Many finance clients prefer not to be named, so anonymized case studies, such as 'a Series A SaaS company in Austin', are common. Change only the details that would identify the client. If an example is illustrative rather than a real engagement, label it clearly and never present it as a real client.

Do FTC rules apply to testimonials on a fractional CFO website?

The FTC's Endorsement Guides say endorsements must be honest and reflect the endorser's real opinion, and unexpected connections such as payment or family ties should be disclosed. The FTC's reviews and testimonials rule Q&A says testimonials on a business's own website are its responsibility. Check current FTC guidance and take legal advice if unsure.

Should fractional CFO case studies include numbers?

Use numbers when they're real, from the engagement, and you can back them up. Give context such as the starting point and the work involved, and don't imply one client's results are what everyone can expect. The FTC says an ad must make clear the generally expected results if an endorser's experience isn't typical.
